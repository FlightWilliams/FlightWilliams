# Lantern Oath · The One Who Came Back

By Will Wang. Complete story manuscript, including the ending.

## Your Missing-Person File

When you wake, Noah, the Night Watch, is wringing out your coat. Blue thread mends one cuff. A damp photograph has been pushed into a pocket: you, standing in front of a group wrapped in blankets. You recognize your face, but none of the people behind it.

“You came back along the harbor road,” Noah says, pouring half a cup of hot tea. “Collapsed at the door. I got you inside. That's all I saw.”

This is the duty room in Fogtown. You get up to look for a way out. Noah does not stop you, but hands you a file. You are the missing person. The date of disappearance is tomorrow. Water has blurred the reporting person's signature, leaving a long hooked final stroke.

“Is someone threatening me?”

“Someone reported you missing,” Noah says carefully. “We don't know who. The date might be a mistake. It isn't evidence that you're going to die.”

The wall clock reads 03:17. Noah explains that the town has been caught in this night since the fire at the Ash Works. Some roads lead back to their starting point. People's memories do not survive intact. He does not know how it began. He only knows that paper and recordings are more dependable than memory.

You study the photograph. Those people are standing. So are you. At least when it was taken, you were alive.

“I need to find where this was taken, and who filed this report.”

Noah spreads out a street map. The post office is two streets away; the file was delivered from there.

“Start with the dispatch record. Follow the road you can prove you took. Don't chase every frightening rumor. When people disagree, use the cards to lay out what happened first. Write down their accounts.”

You finish the tea and put the photograph and file inside your coat. This time, you know what you are going out to do.

## 1 · Fogtown

### Ask Who Actually Saw You

The cook recognizes your coat and brings soup to a small table by the window. He served breakfast to evacuees on the night of the fire. Many travelers were as soaked as you.

“Then you saw me?”

“I saw that coat. I won't swear to the face. It was dark, and everyone had their head down.”

Instead of pressing him, you lay out the photograph. He recognizes the barrier in the background: a transfer point on the harbor road, not the edge of town. He marks it in your file and offers to write down what he is sure he saw.

At the post office, Nils, the Letter Receiver, is sorting the night's undelivered mail. He can trace a transit mark. He cannot guess who sent an envelope without a return address.

“Don't start by deciding someone is hunting you,” Nils says. “Find out which way you came back. Then find out which way the letter came. When the routes meet, we'll have something.”

You circle the transfer point and post office on Noah's map. Now you must ask patrols, porters and the people by the bridge what they actually saw: someone disappearing, or someone returning without being recorded.

### The Letter Passed Through the Bell Tower

By the time the final witness statement reaches the post office, Nils has cleared a counter for you. You lay out the accounts. Some people saw you at the evacuation point. Others saw you returning from the harbor. Nobody actually saw you disappear.

“So the file proves someone was looking for me. It doesn't prove I died.” You write that on the first page.

From a fold in the original envelope, Nils retrieves a short length of blue thread. Its backward knot matches the mend in your cuff. The sender may have handled your coat. That is a reason to investigate, not enough to name them.

He turns the envelope over and shows you a legible transit stamp: Bell Tower Night Office. The tower collected emergency recordings and forwarded mail for places cut off by the fire. It should have a record of accepting this envelope.

Before you leave, the cook packs soup into a lidded tin. You remember to thank him this time.

You now know that you once came back. The next step is to check the tower's calls and forwarding records, not fear the word “tomorrow.” Somewhere in those records may be the connection between you and the people in blankets.

## 2 · Bell Tower

### Separate Before and After the Outage

In the tower's night office, a recording clerk uses a pencil to tighten a loose reel. After checking the transit stamp, she finds the incoming-mail ledger and sets a stack of emergency-call records before you.

“The letter was forwarded after the outage. To find what you did before that, we need the earlier recordings.”

She presses Play. A hoarse, urgent voice tells people to leave the Ash Works by its service passage, not the main gate, and continue to the harbor. Something about the speaker's phrasing makes you sit still. It sounds like you.

You ask whether the machine knows the future. She shakes her head and points to the receiving line and reel order. “This is a call recorded when it happened. It isn't a prophecy. Find whoever answered it before you decide whose voice it is.”

The tower clock has stopped at 03:17 too. She sets its timings aside and keeps the shift handover sheet, call-connection log and reel number. Those can be checked against one another without trusting anyone's memory of how many bells rang.

You sit down and arrange cards for the alarm, the evacuation and the outage. You need to locate the call, understand why the speaker knew the service passage, and decide what it really proves about your part in that night.

### The Call Came from the Ash Works

The recordings finally fall into order. The Ash Works raised a fire alarm. Someone then called from the east-furnace duty room to request the service exit. At 03:17 the power failed and the clock stopped; later bell timings cannot be treated as precise.

The clerk records you reading an address and plays the two voices together. The pauses, corrections and pronunciation are close, but she writes a careful conclusion: probably the same speaker, requiring confirmation at the scene.

The telephone ledger provides firmer evidence: the east-furnace duty-room extension and an evacuation route from the service passage to the harbor. The original envelope arrived in an emergency mailbag after the outage. The tower only forwarded it to Fogtown's post office. The tower has no reliable name for its sender.

“Directing an evacuation doesn't make someone the person who started the fire,” the clerk says. You do not argue. Hearing what might be your own voice, you had begun to fear exactly that.

She clips the transcript into your file. On the map you follow the service passage as far as the factory gate. The next person you need is whoever held that gate on the night of the fire. They may be able to tell you whether you helped people escape, or left some other trouble behind.

## 3 · Ash Works

### The Door She Held Open

Half a pot of cabbage soup remains in the Ash Works gatehouse. The Fire Warden serves you a bowl and reads the tower's transcript. She nods at the words “service passage.”

“You said that. I heard you.”

Rolling up her sleeve, she shows you an old burn. When the furnace area caught fire, she stayed to hold the fire door so people could reach the service passage. You took a hand lamp and led the people at the rear toward the harbor.

You touch the blue stitching on your coat. She remembers the torn cuff catching on a doorway hook. She gave you a needle and blue thread; unwilling to delay the evacuation, you patched it yourself, finishing with your usual backward knot.

“Then I wasn't—”

“I saw you helping people,” she finishes. “But I didn't go to the harbor. I can't promise what happened after that. Don't stop investigating on my word, and don't blame yourself on the strength of one recording.”

She lays a shift sheet and evacuation plan on the table. The people who issued tools, opened the passage and counted the evacuees each saw part of the night. Together their accounts can establish when the last group left.

The soup is still too hot. You steady the bowl. For the first time, this feels like something you may actually be able to finish.

### They Saw You Lead People Out

The duty record, tool-issue sheet and photograph from the evacuation point are finally side by side. You recognize the hand lamp. You signed for it. The people behind you are the last group to come through the service passage.

The photograph was taken outside the factory gate. The handover slip names their destination: the final evacuation boat at the harbor. The witnesses' signatures agree. Nobody was lost from the group before it left the factory.

You cross out “possibly involved in starting the fire,” which you had written in the margin. Not because someone reassured you, but because the records place you collecting guide equipment when the alarm was raised and helping with the evacuation afterward.

The Fire Warden takes your empty bowl and offers more soup. You say you should reach the harbor while the route is clear. She wraps a copy of the handover slip in oilpaper to keep it dry.

“I couldn't see beyond the gate,” she says. “Find out who that boat took. Don't settle for knowing it sailed.”

You straighten the blue-mended cuff. This place has answered what you did during the fire. Next you must find whether those people boarded, and why you ended up alone in Fogtown.

## 4 · Sunken Harbor

### No Seat Is Not the Same as No Passenger

The harbor office desk is so narrow that you must move a coil of rescue rope before laying out the factory handover slip. The clerk finds the right sailing, but not your name on its numbered passenger list.

You stare at the blank space. The fear you had begun to put down returns.

Eli, the Lighthouse Keeper, comes in and sets a half-repaired lamp on the floor. He remembers that a stretcher needed extra room. You gave up your numbered seat and stood beside the crew, helping support the passengers. He saw you board. Memory alone cannot prove where you got off.

“This list was copied from the seats. Helpers are easy to miss.” Eli turns it over. “Check the crew's handover record. Don't let an empty box write your ending.”

You ask about the strange signal received by the lighthouse. He points to the connection book: a recording marked as coming from the White Room triggers the watch lamp's alert. It is a message whose source needs checking, not somebody under the water calling you in.

First you must reconcile the last boat's passengers, helpers and landing. Only then can you tell whether the records lost a name, or whether someone truly failed to reach the shore.

### The Helper's Entry Was Left Out

The captain sets the seat list aside and finds the crew's retained working copy. Your name is on the back: assisting injured travelers, aboard with the final group, stationed at the stern.

The two sheets do not describe an extra person or a missing body. When someone copied the evacuation figures, they used only the numbered seats and omitted you from the count. You circle the error and ask the captain to sign the correction.

The landing log places the boat at the transfer pier below Snow Station, where its passengers were handed over to station staff. Eli writes down the handover reference. He reminds you that this proves the boat reached land, not that every passenger entered the station or reached reception. Those arrivals require the station's receipt.

You extend the route northward on your map. The fire, the factory departure and the boat journey now join together. The sea has not hidden a second identity. You have been trying to find yourself on a form with no space for a helper.

The captain tightens the strap around your file. “If you find the station copy, send word.”

You promise. You are going to Snow Station to find a record of safe arrival, not to look for proof that you drowned.

## 5 · Snow Station

### Match the People Who Arrived

The station attendant takes the handover reference and asks you to sit by the radiator. Your fingers are too cold to separate the pages. He puts a mint and a cup of hot water beside you.

“The last boat? Those people were registered here. Wrong book, though.”

He takes you to the transfer window. The pier recorded disembarkation; the station recorded entry; reception kept an arrival receipt. The three must not be treated as one list. Otherwise a missing entry can turn somebody still making their way to reception into a missing person.

As you compare names, the radio plays an old message: “Do not report yourself missing again. Bring the arrival receipt.” The voice sounds like you. The recorded source is the White Room. You are no longer willing to identify someone by sound alone, but at least the message says exactly what to look for.

The attendant lowers the volume without switching it off. “Find the receipt first. Then ask who sent that. Being right about one thing doesn't mean a voice knows everything.”

You set the pier handover slip on the counter and start tracing the group after it landed. If you reached the White Room too, you can place a complete living journey beside the missing-person report and see where the account went wrong.

### The Last Group Reached the Station

The arrival receipt is found in reception's retained ledger. All the boat's passengers are there, each with a receiving signature. Your name appears below them under “accompanying guide.” After disembarking, you brought the last group into Snow Station. A departure entry records that you later took the footpath toward the White Room.

The attendant also finds a medical note. Blue stitching at your cuff. Furnace ash on your hands. Exhausted enough to fall asleep as soon as you sat down. These ordinary details match what people at the factory and pier recorded independently. You are not a guess on a list. People in different places actually received you.

“Nobody from that group was left on the boat,” the attendant says.

You put that in a message for the captain and add a line to your own file: arrival at Snow Station confirmed. As the tension leaves your chest, you realize you are still holding the mint.

The questions left are more precise. If you arrived safely, why did the White Room send a report that you were missing? Why did the broadcast warn you not to file another one?

The station gives you a receipt copy with the White Room's outer reception entrance and registration window marked on the back. You have the final document needed to corroborate the factory handover and crew record. This time you will bring evidence, not only your name.

## 6 · White Room

### Put the Whole Journey on the Table

An ink pad sits on the White Room reception desk. Instead of giving your name first, you lay out the factory handover slip, the crew's working copy and the station arrival receipt. Their different signatures finally join one person's journey all the way to this room.

The clerk reads slowly. He remembers the blue-mended coat, but will not identify the sender on that alone. He retrieves the retained copy of the original report and asks you to compare its filing time, delivery route and signature before another assumption is copied as fact.

“Can you send things into the past?”

He points to the return slot in the wall. “We've tested it. Papers and recordings put through there appear at receiving points earlier in this same night. People cannot go through. The forwarded envelope at the Bell Tower and the station broadcast can both be traced from here.”

You ask whether that means filing another missing-person report.

“Not yet,” he says. “You already have an arrival record. Find out why the first report was written before deciding you need another.”

There are two routes out of the corridor. The inner passage returns to Fogtown. The outer one is the evacuees' way out. You circle the reception entrance marked by the station. This time you will hear the accounts and check the originals before choosing a door.

### No More Missing-Person Appeals

When the final records have been checked, Evan slides the report's carbon copy toward you. The second sheet preserves the complete signature, even a word corrected halfway through writing it.

You were the reporting person.

You put the photograph and blue thread beside it. The clerk explains that on your previous visit you had brought none of the route's corroborating documents. You remembered escorting people, but did not know where they had ended up. Afraid they were still waiting, and afraid you would be forgotten yourself, you filed an appeal to find you. You hoped its recipient would retrace the journey.

Your finger follows the long hook at the end of the signature. The familiar pressure of your own wrist brings back the moment of writing it.

“I chose that date,” you say. “I thought putting tomorrow would give whoever came to help one more day.”

It was never a prediction of death. It was a frightened, exhausted person's imperfect request for help. You remember the earlier attempt: you had cut blue thread from the mended cuff and tied your usual backward knot as a way to identify the sender. The photograph, report and envelope went through the return slot and were forwarded through the Bell Tower to Fogtown. They arrived earlier in the night than the person who had written them. That was the file beside you when you woke.

“What did I do after sending it?”

The clerk points to the inner passage. “Went back to wait for someone to find you. Later you returned with the same report. You kept replacing the missing-person form without bringing the proof missing from the route.”

This time, more than your own signature lies on the desk. Factory witnesses place you guiding the group out. The crew confirms you stayed aboard to help after giving up your seat. Station staff received both you and the travelers safely. Their independent accounts agree. You helped them, and you too reached the end of the dangerous part of the journey.

You do not complete another appeal. In the old file's “Found” field, you record where you arrived and what happened, and ask the clerk to attach the three sets of evidence. He closes the file and points to the outer door marked on the station map: the route used by the evacuees. Before, you had not known where it led and had gone back to Fogtown to wait. Now you know the group arrived, and which way you can go.

The station warning now has a source too. You speak into the recorder: “Do not report yourself missing again. Bring the arrival receipt.” You send the recording and a route copy through the slot, addressed to Snow Station. What you send back is usable guidance, not another appeal asking someone else to find you.

The outer door opens. Morning light reaches the end of the corridor. Outside, you hear somebody taking down a road barrier. The wind is cold and unmistakable against your face.

You can leave.

Before you go, you carry the completed file back to Fogtown. Seeing the arrival receipt, Noah gets up to fetch a chair. You tell him that you wrote the report, the route is now established, and the last group has been found.

“Still want that tea?”

“Yes. Then I'll be on my way.”

You open the file for him without hiding the reporting person's name. Outside, the sky is growing light. If you travel this route again, it will be to bring the answer to people still waiting for news, not because you no longer know who you are.

## Bring the Answer Back

Noah puts the completed file in the middle of the desk. The missing-person report has not been burned or replaced with a clean sheet. Your fear, the date you chose and the evidence later added to correct the account all remain in it.

“The harbor is waiting for word,” you say. “I promised the people at the factory too.”

He gives you a cup with a lid so you will not spill the tea. “At least this time you're not looking for yourself.”

You nod. You have seen morning light through the outer door. You came back to return the findings to witnesses and check experiences that remain unclear. The old records still say 03:17. You know that was when the power failed. You no longer have to read every page as a prediction of your own fate.

You pack the verified accounts. Whoever wants to come can travel with you; those who stay can still receive the news. You do not have to begin with an empty form every time.
