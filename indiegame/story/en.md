# Lantern Oath · An Open Case

By Will Wang. Complete story manuscript, including the ending.

## The Stranger in the Duty Room

Wet wool is the first thing you smell.
Your coat hangs over a chair. Water runs from its cuff into a tin basin, already half an inch deep. Your shoes are clean. You cannot remember the road you took, or who helped you out of the coat.
Across the desk, a man is mending a glove. His uniform collar has worn pale. He holds a thread between his lips. A radio murmurs static. A kettle sits on the stove with newspaper stuffed into its spout.
“Awake? Help yourself to a cup. Keep the chipped side away from your mouth.” He tucks the needle into his sleeve.
You do not drink. Tea has left a ring in the cup. It smells of cold iron.
“I want to leave.”
“Good. You haven't decided to take my job.”
He smiles, turns the glove over, and unpicks the two stitches he has just made. You wait for more. Instead, he pushes a thin file across the desk. Damp has glued its pages together. You separate them with your thumb and find your photograph.
You are due to go missing tomorrow. The reporting person’s name is folded into the cover. Only a heavily hooked final stroke shows.
“The date is wrong.”
“I said that too.” The Night Watch bends over his thread.
The clock on the wall reads 03:17. A drop falls from your sleeve into the basin. A moment later, the radio repeats the sound.
He puts the sewing away and lays a soft-edged deck of cards between you. The glove has turned inside out. On its lining is the wet thumbprint you have just left on the file.
The water around it has dried.

## 1 · Fogtown

### A Breakfast Gone Cold

Outside the duty room, the first thing you notice is the smell of beans. The cook across the street has his door half open. He lifts the lid just far enough to let the steam warm his reddened nose.
“Still here?” he asks.
“Have we met?”
“I run a business. I say that to anyone standing outside without buying.”
He hands you an enamel cup with a chip in its blue rim. The soup is hot. Pepper catches your throat. Laughing, he thumps the radio into life. A woman in the weather forecast is reading out your name.
He turns it down at once.
“Missing people at breakfast. They ought to think about appetites.”
You ask about the outage. He says Wednesday. The man buying cigarettes next door says Sunday. Soon they are arguing about an unpaid debt. Both agree that the clock stopped at 03:17.
You set down the cup. Sixteen keys hang beneath the counter. The last is dripping onto the floor. The cook follows your gaze. His smile fades.
“Leave that one. They haven't fitted its lock yet.”

### An Unopened Letter

As you put away the last card, the cook replaces the lid on his pot. The fog has thinned enough to reveal the crooked post-office sign across the street.
An unsealed envelope lies on the counter. Your name is in the sender field. Inside is a short piece of blue thread. Its knot is tied backward, a habit you learned badly as a child and never corrected. The postmark is dated tomorrow, but its ink has soaked deep into the paper.
“Our postman always takes his time,” the cook says.
“This seems fairly quick.”
“That's what bothers me.”
Someone pauses outside, willing to walk a little of the road with you. You make room in the doorway and pocket the envelope. The thread catches on your torn cuff. One small tug pulls the edges neatly together.
On your way out, the cook tosses you the wet key. You do not ask whether the lock has been fitted.
The road to the bell tower runs behind the post office. A few steps along it, you turn to return the cup. The cook has already washed it and placed it upside down on the shelf.
You look at your hand. Your cup is still warm.

## 2 · Bell Tower

### Before the Chime

The wooden banister is polished with old grease. As you climb, each footstep receives an answer from upstairs, half a beat late.
In the duty room, a thin woman is changing the tape in a recorder. She turns a spool with a pencil. Two peeled orange segments wait beside her elbow.
“Want one?”
You decline. She eats it herself and winces at the sourness.
“Good. Someone still trusts me.”
You show her the envelope. At the sight of the postmark, she sweeps aside the peel to make a clean space.
“That goes upstairs. We deal in sounds, not dates.”
The recorder clicks. Before you can speak, your voice on the tape asks, “Who's upstairs?”
Without looking at the machine, she opens a drawer, takes out a printed interview sheet and crosses off the first question.
“You usually wait a little longer.”
At the window, you can see the empty bell frame overhead. A broken rope hangs from the beam. Down in the alley, traders begin packing their stalls as though the hour has sounded.
She offers you the headphones and untangles the cable from your collar. Her fingers are cold.
“Stop at the sixteenth statement. Don't count along with the last chime.”

### The Plug You Didn't Pull

The footsteps in the stairwell stop at last. You wait. Nobody opens the door.
The thin woman packs the tape into a cardboard box and writes the Ash Works address on its lid. Her pencil is a stub. It breaks halfway through the building number. You offer your pen. She takes it so quickly she seems to have been waiting for your hand.
“They repair things there. People too. Keep your expectations sensible.”
You close the box. A small cough comes from inside.
She looks up. “Nothing to worry about. The tape is wound too tight.”
The White Room address is written inside the lid, its last digit retraced. Your hand stops over the pen. The same color of ink stains your fingertip.
You reach to unplug the recorder. Your fingers find a cable cut off close to the casing. Dust has settled into the end. It was cut a long time ago.
Outside, a companion takes the heavy box from you. Through the cardboard, you hear your recorded voice thank them.
They look at you.
You haven't spoken yet.

## 3 · Ash Works

### The Fire Warden's Lunch

Cabbage soup scents the gatehouse at Ash Works. When you push the door open, the Fire Warden is skimming fat from the pot, an old raincoat spread across her knees. There is no fire in the stove. The soup keeps boiling.
“Bowls are up there.”
You hesitate. She looks from you to the pot.
“It's bad, I'll grant you. Nothing added to make it worse.”
You take a bowl. Its heat loosens your stiff fingers. She accepts the tape box, wipes coal dust from its base and signs the handover book. As the nib moves across the page, you hear a brief chime.
She pauses, then finishes her signature.
After lunch, she rolls up her sleeves to inspect the furnace door. Every seal is straight, every corner pressed down twice. You ask why she still works here after twenty-seven years of closure.
“The machines stopped. They never settled the wages. Besides, what's in there hasn't heard about the closure.”
Something taps from deep inside the furnace.
She puts down her spoon and draws on insulated gloves. The humor leaves her face. A fine line of blue stitching repairs one glove at the cuff.
“Finish your soup and leave the bowl,” she says. “Whatever name it starts calling.”

### After the Heat

The last transfer slip goes into the drawer. At last, the furnace falls quiet. The Fire Warden removes one glove and sits at the table until her shoulders begin to ease.
You mean to ask what is inside. Instead, you ask whether there is any soup left.
She looks at you and pushes the pot closer.
“Cold. Don't complain.”
The disconnected copier feeds out a photograph. The paper is warm. It shows a quay at Sunken Harbor. She picks a flake of ash from one corner, revealing a blue-rimmed cup upside down on the rail.
You recognize the chip. In the photographed water, a bell-tower window is reflected.
“Take the left road,” she says. “When the water rises, don't ask anyone the time.”
She presses a spool of blue thread into your palm and cuts a length before you can refuse. “For torn clothes. You needn’t hold a funeral for the place.”
Before leaving, you put your bowl in the sink. The water in the bottom moves away from the drain. Slowly, it climbs the bowl and washes off your fingerprints.

## 4 · Sunken Harbor

### The Reply Arrives First

There is no sea breeze at Sunken Harbor, but salt stings your lips. Water lies beneath the quay. Each step brings a wet answering step from below.
The Lighthouse Warden sits on a low stool, taking apart a navigation lamp green with corrosion. He sorts the screws by size inside a tobacco tin. A kettle boils beside him, a stick wedged in its spout to keep it quiet.
“How do you know when it's ready?”
“It starts swearing.”
You look at him. He removes another screw. One corner of his mouth moves.
“Given enough time, so do I.”
You offer the Ash Works photograph. He leaves it in your hand and raises the wick, shining through the scratched glass. The photographed water stirs.
A beam appears beneath the boards: cold white, straight through the black water. Only then does the lamp in his hands light up.
He checks his watch and notes a difference of one minute, adding a mark beside an older entry.
“It used to be a few seconds.”
The kettle gives a sharp hiss. You reach for it. He catches your wrist with a warm, steady hand.
“Let it finish.”

### The Third Passenger

The Warden fits the lamp glass back into place and wipes it with his cuff. No further light comes from below. He does not seem relieved. He only presses harder on the last mark in his book.
A boat waits at the steps. The ferryman is crouched over a shoe repair. The needle squeaks as it passes through the thick leather. He takes the harbor ledger, finds the missing-passenger page and asks whether the photograph is yours.
Your face. Another name.
Behind you, someone quietly says a third name. It sounds familiar. When you turn, they deny having spoken.
The ferryman puts on his shoe and tests it.
“One face won't get you two tickets.”
Before you can answer, a telegraph starts clattering farther along the quay. Its paper unrolls onto the floor. It gives the Snow Station address and three words: DO NOT ANSWER.
A thumb has smeared the ink over the sender’s name. You swallow your answer.
The ferryman waits too long, then asks you the question again.

## 5 · Snow Station

### A Seat for the Late Arrival

The waiting-room radiator is too hot to touch. A film of ice coats the inside of the window. The attendant crouches beside a bench, folding old newspaper beneath its short leg. When he stands, you see that he has saved the cleanest seat for you.
“How late is the train?”
“Depends how long you sit.”
He finds a peppermint in a tin, considers it, then replaces it with two. The brittle wrappers crackle in his fingers.
A service with no destination is announced over the loudspeaker. He adds a routine mark to the blackboard and turns on the radio. Your distress call forces its way through the speaker, broken by hard breathing.
He lowers the volume slowly.
“We can make out the address this time.”
At the last digit, the voice hesitates and repeats it. You recognize the pause. That is how you read the address inside the box lid.
The switch has stayed on receive.
Your recorded voice gives the White Room building number. As the final digit sounds, his fingers stop on the dial. You think he has heard something. Then he shows you his open hand.
The sweets have melted. A sodden ticket lies between their two wrappers.
The departure station is Fogtown.

### A Coat You Needn't Return

There is no train at the end of the platform. Only a lamp bent beneath the snow. In its light, you spread out the last statement. It describes the match you have just played, including a move you thought better of making.
The time is 03:17.
The attendant brushes snow from your shoulders and offers you his coat. You tell him you have one. He feels the thin material, makes no argument and pins a sewing needle to your cuff.
“If a seam goes, mend it. Don't leave it until you get back.”
You ask how he will keep warm.
He nods toward the waiting room. “I've got the radiator. Hot as anything.”
The small door to the White Room stands behind the station building. No handle, just an old keyhole. As you lift the Fogtown key toward it, the bolt withdraws by itself.
Behind you, the bench creaks.
The attendant is folding paper under its leg for the next arrival.

## 6 · White Room

### The Opposite Chair

The White Room smells of a freshly mopped floor. It has no windows, though damp marks ring the walls. Three enamel cups stand on the table. Their water is still. The shadows at their bases move independently.
The recording officer pulls out a chair. His shirt is neatly pressed. A rusted paper clip is pinned to one cuff.
“Keep your coat on. You won't be sitting long.”
You ask what counts as long. He opens the file instead of answering and places a photograph in the middle of the table. You are seated in this chair. The person behind you has been cropped in half.
Missing person. Witness. Recording officer. Your name fills all three fields. The last stroke in the reporting field ends in a hook. You made the same mark a moment ago, finishing the address for him.
You touch the paper. The smell of cabbage soup from Ash Works rises briefly between you. He smells it too. He swallows and straightens the pens.
“Can I go when we're finished?”
“You can leave this room.”
You look at him. He crosses out the sentence and writes it again without changing a word.
A light comes on behind the door. Cloth brushes against a chair back. The empty seat opposite you begins sliding away from the table.
At last, he puts down his pen.
“Now I'd like you to hear the other account.”

### The Cup Is Still Warm

The last card lands. Somewhere in the White Room, a kettle comes to the boil. The recording officer looks down at the table. He makes no move to switch it off.
The recorder at the corner of the desk begins turning. Someone is breathing hard, asking whether anyone can hear. You know that breath. At Snow Station, you turned it down because it was too loud.
“Give them the address.” Into the microphone, you read the White Room building number. You stumble over the last digit and repeat it. The person on the tape pauses in the same place.
You let go of the microphone. Your palm is wet.
The officer offers a blank missing-person form. “Nobody goes looking until somebody reports it.”
The photograph is yours. The form is empty. You remember everyone who has held a door or saved a meal for you, and write your name. At the date, you hesitate. Then you put tomorrow. At least there is still a day, you think.
The last stroke of the reporting person’s name ends in a hook you cannot quite control.
Afraid your earlier self will answer the ferryman, you write the Snow Station address and DO NOT ANSWER on telegraph paper. Your thumb catches the wet ink, smearing the sender’s name.
The Fire Warden’s blue thread is still in your pocket. You cut a length, tie the backward knot, and put it in an envelope bearing your name. The wet key holds down its flap. You push everything, with the report, into the return slot in the wall, hoping to leave a route for someone who cannot get out.
Inside the metal chute, a spoon taps the edge of a pot. Then the cook’s weather forecast begins reading your name.
You reach back in. Your fingers find only damp metal.
The wet mark retreats down the wall, revealing a narrow door. The form has left the desk. Its carbon copy is the first page you opened when you woke.
“I sent it back,” you say.
The officer looks at the ink on your hand. He does not say, “At last.”
You open the door.
In the Fogtown duty room, the Night Watch is pouring water. The clock reads 03:17. He looks at your face, then at the space behind you. The kettle lingers above the rim.
“Name?”
You do not answer at once. The missing-person file is still dated tomorrow, but a soup stain marks one corner. You place the things you have brought back over the photograph.
“How many chairs do you have?”
He counts them. The room is quiet. You hear someone trying not to laugh outside the door.
“One short today, I think.”
You pull out a chair for whoever has followed you and hang your coat over the chair where you first found it. Its wooden legs scrape the floor with an ordinary, ugly sound. Your hand stops at the familiarity of it.
You know who sent the letter. Nobody has accounted for the two stitches the Night Watch unpicked, the print inside his glove, or the light beneath the water.
The fog has not cleared. As you sit, the cup burns your palm. The missing blue enamel is returning. This time, you leave the reporting person’s name unfolded.

## The Tea Is Still Warm

The tin basin is where you left it. So is your coat, with a length of blue thread mending its cuff. Hearing the chair scrape, the Night Watch counts the people in the doorway and brings out more cups.
“More mouths. I'll have to water it down.”
The tea smells of iron. The clock still reads 03:17. This time, the radio does not speak ahead of you. You open the file without hiding the reporting field. It is still your name.
“I want to leave,” you say.
He waits with the needle between his fingers.
“I know. Put your coat on first.”
